How the test was done

  1. Downloaded the GSE5281 series matrix from NCBI GEO and kept the hippocampus samples.
  2. Found the probes for each gene on the Affymetrix HG-U133 Plus 2.0 chip. LINC03007 and LINC02987 have newer names than the chip annotation, so they were matched by NCBI Gene ID (646588 and 101927151).
  3. Turned the MAS5 values into log2 and standardised each gene within the dataset. The GSE48350 means could not be reused because the data is on a different scale.
  4. Computed the panel score for every sample without looking at the labels, then compared scores and labels.
  5. Nothing about the panel (genes, directions, scoring) was changed after seeing the results.

The script is validation/validate_gse5281.py in the repository and runs in about a second after the 36 MB download.

What could explain it

  • Overfitting. The panel was chosen from about 20,000 genes using only 37 samples. With that many genes, some will separate a small dataset by chance.
  • Batch effects. In GSE48350, disease status and processing batch overlap, so some “Alzheimer’s” genes may really be batch genes.
  • Different material. GSE5281 used laser-captured CA1 neurons, while GSE48350 used whole hippocampus tissue. Genes from glia or other cells may behave differently. This makes the test harder, but a robust marker should still show the same direction.
  • Small test set. 23 samples gives a wide interval (see above), but even the top of that interval is close to chance.

What this means for the project

The website still works as a teaching tool: it shows how a gene panel score is built, and how to check a panel honestly. A next step would be to choose genes on several datasets together and test on another one, such as whole-tissue hippocampus data from a third study.
